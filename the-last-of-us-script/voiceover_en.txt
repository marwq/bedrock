One night, the world as we knew it simply ended. Not because of a war. Not because of a meteor. Because of a fungus. Twenty years later, all that's left of America is ruins, swallowed by grass and vines. People hide behind walls, fight over the last bullets, and fear each other as much as the monsters. And in the middle of this world, one tired, bitter man gets a job that seems simple at first. Take a fourteen-year-old girl across the entire country. A girl who might hold the key to saving humanity. This is The Last of Us — one of the most powerful stories ever told in a video game. Naughty Dog released the game in 2013; it won hundreds of awards, and ten years later it became a TV series. It has everything: loss, betrayal, monsters, people scarier than monsters, and an ending fans still argue about. If you've never played it, don't worry — you're about to hear the whole story from start to finish. And it begins in Texas.

A suburb of Austin, 2013. Joel Miller is a construction worker and a single dad. He's raising his twelve-year-old daughter, Sarah. It's Joel's birthday, but he gets home late, completely worn out. Sarah is still up — she waited for him so she could give him a present. It's a wristwatch. Joel pretends to be strict and asks where she got the money, and Sarah just jokes her way out of it. They fall asleep together on the couch, watching an old movie. Joel carries his daughter to bed. It's the last peaceful evening of their lives. From here on, we see the night through Sarah's eyes. In the middle of the night, a phone call wakes Sarah up. It's Uncle Tommy, Joel's younger brother. His voice is shaking; he asks where her dad is — and the line goes dead. Sarah walks through the empty house, calling for her dad, but nobody answers. Outside, sirens wail as police cars race past, one after another. A breaking news report talks about overflowing hospitals and people attacking strangers. Then an explosion goes off somewhere close. Joel bursts in — pale, with a pistol in his hand. Before he can explain anything, the neighbor starts smashing through the glass door. The neighbor isn't human anymore: he wheezes, twitches, and lunges at them. Joel fires. Sarah is terrified, and her father keeps saying the man was sick and they have to leave right now. Tommy's pickup screeches to a stop outside. The three of them try to get out of town. All around them is madness: houses burning, people running, some attacking others. On the roadside, a father holding a child waves desperately, begging them to stop. Tommy wants to pull over, but Joel won't budge: they have a kid with them, they can't take the risk. Remember this moment — it will come back at the very end of the story. The highway is jammed, and the whole city is in a panic. Tommy cuts through downtown, but it's even worse there. A gas station blows up nearby, and a second later another car slams into their truck at full speed. Sarah hurts her leg and can't walk. Joel picks her up, and they run through a crowd of people gone mad. The infected are everywhere, attacking anyone in their path. Tommy stays behind to hold them off and yells at his brother to run and not look back. On the edge of town, a soldier stops Joel and his daughter. Joel begs for help, but the soldier gets an order over the radio. The order is simple and terrible: no one gets out. The soldier opens fire. Joel tumbles down the embankment, holding Sarah tight. At the last second, Tommy appears and kills the soldier. But it's already too late. Sarah has been shot. Joel begs her to hold on, but she dies in his arms. That night, he lost everything. On his wrist, he still wears the watch — his daughter's last gift. He'll wear it for the next twenty years. It's the only thread tying him to his old life.

The culprit was Cordyceps — a parasitic fungus. In real life, this fungus infects ants and literally takes control of their behavior. In the world of the game, the fungus mutated and learned to infect humans. One bite is enough — and within a day or two, a person is no longer themselves. The fungus takes over the brain and turns its victim into a violent creature that attacks everyone around it. Worse still, inside abandoned buildings the fungus spreads and fills the air with spores, and without a gas mask you won't survive. That's why every dark basement in this game is a test of nerve. Over time, the infected change. The first stage, Runners, still look human: fast, frenzied, and they attack in packs. Months later, the fungus bursts through the skull and blinds its host. That's how you get Clickers — the most terrifying creatures in the game. They can't see, so they hunt by sound, making their signature clicking noise, like bats. If a Clicker grabs you, you're almost certainly dead. And the oldest infected, Bloaters, wear the fungus like armor and hurl toxic spore sacks at their enemies. The outbreak swept across the planet at terrifying speed. The government fell. Survivors were herded into quarantine zones — walled-off cities run by the military. Inside, there are curfews, food rations, and execution at the slightest suspicion of infection. Fighting the military is a resistance group called the Fireflies. They believe that one day they'll find a cure and give people their normal lives back. And beyond the walls, in the wild, everyone survives however they can: bandits, loners, small communities. Twenty years have passed since that terrible night.

Boston, 2033. The city is split by a wall: inside, hunger, rations, and military patrols; outside, ruins and the infected. Joel has grown older and harder; there's nothing left of the caring father he used to be. He's a smuggler now, moving guns, pills, and anything people will pay for across the wall. His partner is Tess — tough, smart, and sharp-tongued. There's clearly more than business between them, but they both forgot how to talk about feelings a long time ago. One day, Tess comes back beaten up. A dealer named Robert set her up and stole their shipment of guns. Joel and Tess go after him. Smugglers know secret routes under the wall that the military never even suspects. They corner Robert in a warehouse by the docks. Terrified, Robert confesses: the guns are gone — he sold them to the Fireflies. Tess doesn't forgive betrayal. And then Marlene, leader of the Boston Fireflies, steps out of the shadows. She's wounded but perfectly calm, and she has an offer. They need to smuggle some "cargo" out of the city and deliver it to a Firefly team waiting at the Capitol building. The reward: twice as many guns as were stolen from them. Tess agrees to at least take a look at the cargo. The cargo turns out to be a girl. Her name is Ellie, and she's fourteen. She's an orphan who grew up in a military boarding school and has never once set foot outside the wall. Ellie is cheeky, swears worse than most adults, and has no intention of being scared of some grumpy stranger. Marlene has known her since the day she was born, but she can't get her out of the city herself: she's wounded, and the military is right on her heels. Joel isn't happy about it: he's a smuggler, not a babysitter. But a deal is a deal. Tess goes to make sure the guns are real, while Joel and Ellie wait for nightfall in a hideout. Ellie peppers Joel with questions, and he answers in single words — or not at all. He makes it clear right away: they're not friends, this is just a job. Late that evening, Tess returns: the guns are real, and the deal is on.

Under cover of night, the three of them sneak toward the way out of the city. But right at the wall, a military patrol catches them. A soldier checks each of them with an infection scanner. When he gets to Ellie, she pulls a knife and stabs him. A fight breaks out, and Joel has to take down the whole patrol. Tess picks up the fallen scanner — and freezes. Ellie is infected. Joel immediately aims his pistol at the girl. Ellie rolls up her sleeve and shows them the bite. The bite is three weeks old. Anyone else would have turned into a monster long ago, but Ellie is still herself. That's why the Fireflies need her: she's immune, and their scientists hope to use her to make a vaccine. Joel wants to walk away from the whole thing, but Tess talks him into seeing it through. Beyond the wall lies dead Boston. For the first time, Ellie sees the outside world, and she can't take her eyes off it. Everything amazes her: rusted cars, empty shops, signs from a world she never knew. Tess is warmer to the girl than Joel is, and Ellie takes to her. Their path runs through a leaning skyscraper, flooded streets, and a pitch-dark museum. Then, in the darkness, the clicking starts. Clickers hunt by sound, and any noise here could cost you your life. One by one, they creep past the blind monsters, barely breathing. At last, they reach the Capitol. This is where their journey is supposed to end. But the Fireflies who were supposed to meet them are dead. Tess is devastated: was this whole journey for nothing? And then Tess admits something terrible: she's been bitten too. It happened in one of the fights along the way, and unlike Ellie, she isn't immune. And the soldiers are already on their trail. Tess makes a decision. She'll stay behind and hold off the soldiers. She can't be saved anyway: soon, she'll become one of those things herself. Her last request to Joel: get Ellie to Tommy — his brother was a Firefly once and will know where to find them. Tess believes this girl is a real chance, and she won't let Joel argue. Joel and Ellie run. Behind them, gunfire erupts. When it goes quiet, Joel looks back and sees Tess lying on the floor. He has lost another person he cared about. Now it's just Joel and Ellie. He has lost his partner, and she has lost the only adult she was starting to trust.

Tommy lives somewhere far out west, and there's no way to get there on foot. Joel needs a car, and he knows who owes him a favor — an old acquaintance named Bill. Bill lives all alone in a small town he has turned into a fortress. Every street here is booby-trapped, every gate a snare. Joel walks right into one: a rope yanks him upside down. The noise draws the infected. Ellie frees Joel, and then the owner of the town himself comes to the rescue. Bill is paranoid, grumpy, and probably the best survivor in the whole area. He trusts no one, and he's definitely not happy to see guests with a kid. Ellie doesn't back down and snaps back with a sarcastic remark every chance she gets. But a debt is a debt, and Bill agrees to help with the car. Along the way, he shares his philosophy with Joel. Once, he had a partner — someone he took care of. But in this world, getting attached is a weakness that will get you killed. So Bill decided never to get attached to anyone again. Joel says nothing — it seems he lives by the same rules. Searching for a car battery, they fight their way through an abandoned high school crawling with infected. Ellie pitches in, throwing bottles and bricks to distract the infected. And then they find Bill's old partner — Frank. Frank had been bitten, and rather than turn into a monster, he took his own life. Beside him lies a farewell note. In it, Frank writes that he was sick of this town, the walls, the constant fear — and of Bill himself. Bill spent his whole life building walls to keep the pain out, but it found him anyway. For Joel, it's a silent warning: whoever gets attached risks losing everything. In Frank's garage sits a pickup with a charged battery — it looks like Frank was planning to leave too. The infected are closing in, the engine won't start, and all three of them have to push the truck. At the last second, the engine roars to life. Bill lets them keep the truck, and Joel and Ellie drive off. Now Bill doesn't owe anyone anything. On the road, Ellie confesses she swiped an adult magazine from Bill, and tough-guy Joel doesn't know where to look. More importantly, she also took a pistol from Bill — and very soon, it'll come in handy. Next stop: Pittsburgh.

So: Tess is dead, Boston is far behind, and Joel and Ellie finally have wheels. As they drive into the city, a wounded man stands in the middle of the road, begging for help. Joel knows instantly: it's a trap. He hits the gas, and gunmen open fire from an ambush. The truck crashes through a shop window. Joel deals with the attackers, and afterward Ellie asks how he knew it was an ambush. His answer is short: he's been on both sides. That one line says a lot about what he's been doing for the past twenty years. The attackers are Hunters — gangs who took over the city after its people drove out the military. They track down travelers, kill them, and take everything they have. Joel and Ellie have to sneak through neighborhoods where people are hunting them. And this is where it becomes clear: people can be far scarier than any infected. In an abandoned hotel, Joel falls down an elevator shaft and gets cut off from Ellie. He fights his way through a flooded basement full of Clickers and finally makes it back up. But up there, a Hunter jumps him. One more second, and Joel will be strangled. A gunshot rings out. It's Ellie — with that pistol from Bill's. She has killed a man for the first time, and she did it to save Joel. Joel is angry she didn't stay hidden, but Ellie answers simply: she just saved his life. Joel wanted to keep her out of all this, but now he understands: she's not cargo anymore — she's a partner. Soon after, they run into two brothers — Henry and Sam. At first, everyone holds each other at gunpoint, but they quickly realize they share the same enemy. Henry is raising his little brother alone, and he'd do anything for him. Just like Joel once would have for Sarah. Sam is thirteen — quiet, serious, and trying very hard to be brave. Ellie and Sam hit it off right away: for the first time in ages, Ellie has a friend her own age. Henry knows a way out of the city — across a big bridge. But a Hunter armored truck with a machine gun on the roof blocks their way. In the middle of the fight, Henry gets his brother to safety and leaves Joel and Ellie alone under fire. Joel is furious, but deep down he knows: for his own child, he would have done the same. On the bridge, the Hunters catch up with them again, and there's only one way out — jump into the river. Joel goes under and blacks out. Henry pulls him out. Now they're even.

The city is behind them, but safety is still a long way off. Their route runs through the sewers, where an entire community once lived. Notes on the walls tell how people hid down here for years, raised children, and believed they were safe. Until one day, someone left a single door open. Climbing back out, they find themselves in a quiet suburb. In a looted store, Sam spots a toy robot, but Henry tells him to leave it: they only carry what they need. Ellie quietly slips the robot into her own backpack — she wants to surprise Sam with it. In the suburb, a sniper pins them down. Joel circles around behind him while the others take cover behind a car. From the sniper's attic, Joel covers the others as Hunters close in — followed by a whole horde of infected. In the chaos, Sam is left alone for just a few moments. When it's finally over, they find shelter in an empty house. For the first time in days, all four of them can eat and sleep in peace. That night, Sam asks Ellie whether she thinks the infected are still aware — somewhere in there. Ellie doesn't know what to say. But she admits what she fears most of all: being left alone. After Ellie leaves, Sam rolls up his pant leg. There's a fresh bite on his leg. He hasn't told anyone. In the morning, Ellie heads to Sam's room — the robot still in her backpack. But Sam isn't Sam anymore. He lunges at Ellie. A gunshot rings out. It's Henry. He has just shot his own brother. Joel tries to calm him down, but Henry, out of his mind with grief, points the gun at Joel and screams that this is all his fault. And then he turns the gun on himself. It all happens in a matter of seconds. Ellie has lost the first real friend she made on this journey. The toy robot is still lying on the floor. Joel silently leads her away, unable to find the right words. They will never talk about it again.

Months go by. Fall arrives. Joel and Ellie have found a horse, and they've almost stopped bickering. Ellie reads terrible jokes out loud from a battered pun book, and one day stern Joel can't help himself — he laughs. At last, they reach Wyoming, where Tommy, his wife Maria, and their community are restoring an old hydroelectric dam. The brothers haven't seen each other in years, and they didn't part on the best of terms. Before they can really talk, bandits attack the dam, and the brothers have to fight side by side. Tommy has changed: he has a home, a family, and a settlement where the electric lights are on again. For the first time, Ellie sees people who aren't just surviving — they're truly living. And here, Joel does something Ellie never expected. He asks his brother to take her to the Fireflies himself. Tommy used to be one of them and knows the way, and Joel wants out. Tommy doesn't want to take that risk again: he has a wife and people who depend on him. Joel reminds him of an old debt: in the first years after the outbreak, he was the one who kept his little brother alive. But Tommy remembers those years with horror: to survive, the brothers did terrible things. In the end, he agrees. Ellie finds out. She takes a horse and runs off to an abandoned ranch house. Joel finds her, and they have the most honest conversation of their entire journey. Ellie already knows that Joel once had a daughter. The moment she mentions Sarah, Joel cuts her off: she's treading on very thin ice. She tells him she isn't Sarah — but she has lost people too. Everyone she ever cared about has either died or left her. Everyone except Joel. And she won't be any safer with Tommy — just more scared. Joel snaps back: she's not his daughter, and he's not her father. It sounds harsh, but it seems he's mostly trying to convince himself. Their argument is cut short by bandits attacking the ranch. Joel, Ellie, and Tommy fight them off together. Joel sees how confidently Ellie handles herself in a fight, and he realizes she's not that girl from Boston anymore. And when it's over, Joel makes a decision. He'll take her himself. Tommy tells them where to find the Fireflies: at the University of Eastern Colorado. For the first time, Joel stays with Ellie not because of a deal, but because he wants to. The ride to Colorado will take weeks.

The university greets them with silence. Ellie wanders through the abandoned dorms, trying to imagine how ordinary students used to live here. But the Fireflies' lab has been abandoned. The test monkeys have escaped from their open cages, and the scientists are long gone. Joel's face darkens: all those months on the road — and nobody's here. On a desk sits a voice recorder, and the recording makes it clear: the scientists have moved to St. Mary's Hospital in Salt Lake City. They don't get the chance to leave — bandits storm the campus. In the fight, Joel falls from a balcony straight onto a jutting piece of rebar. The metal rod goes right through his side. Ellie helps him up and onto the horse. Now she's the one carrying him, not the other way around. They break out of the campus, but somewhere in a field, Joel falls out of the saddle. Ellie shakes him and begs him to get up. Joel doesn't respond. And winter is only days away.

Now it's Ellie's turn to save Joel. She heads to an abandoned shopping mall to look for medicine. And as she wanders the empty halls, she remembers a very different mall. Boston, shortly before she met Joel. This part of the story is told in the Left Behind expansion, which is now included in The Last of Us Part I. Ellie's best friend is Riley. One day, she vanished for weeks, and when she came back, she admitted she had joined the Fireflies. Riley believes that with the Fireflies, her life will finally mean something. That night, Riley sneaks Ellie into an abandoned shopping mall. There, as if on the last night of childhood, they ride the carousel, goof around in a photo booth, and have a water-gun fight. At a broken arcade machine, Riley acts out the fight on the dead screen while Ellie mashes the buttons and laughs until she cries. Then Riley tells her she's leaving: the Fireflies are sending her to another city. Ellie asks her not to go. And Riley rips the Firefly pendant from her neck — she's staying. Ellie kisses her. But the happiness lasts only a minute. The infected attack them. Both of them are bitten. They know what comes next, and they decide not to leave each other until the end — to simply wait together. Going crazy together, Riley says, would at least be kind of poetic. Riley didn't make it. But Ellie never turned. That's how the Fireflies learned about the girl who was immune. Marlene hid her and started looking for a way to get her to the scientists. Back in the present, Ellie finds a first-aid kit and, with shaking hands, stitches up Joel's wound herself. She's not going to lose anyone else.

Winter comes. In the main game, this is the first time we play as Ellie: now she protects Joel, not the other way around. Joel's wound is infected, he's burning with fever, and without antibiotics he'll die. Ellie hunts with a bow to find food. She wounds a deer and follows its trail for a long time. But by the carcass, two strangers are already waiting for her. The older one introduces himself as David. He says his people are starving, and winter has only just begun. He's polite and reasonable, and he offers a trade: the venison for medicine. While his companion James goes to fetch the antibiotics, the infected attack. David and Ellie fight them off back to back. It's her first fight without Joel, and she holds her own. David clearly admires this girl. And then, almost casually, he mentions that the man she's traveling with killed a lot of his people at the university. Ellie gets a very bad feeling. But David lets her go — with the medicine. Ellie gives Joel the injection, and his fever starts to break. She sits by his side all night without closing her eyes. But David's men are already following her tracks. Ellie leads them away from the hideout where Joel is sleeping. She knows that if they find Joel, helpless and feverish, they'll simply kill him. They catch her. Ellie wakes up in a cage. On the other side of the wall, someone is chopping meat. And suddenly, Ellie realizes whose meat it is. David's people are cannibals. That's why his community hasn't starved. David comes to the cage and talks to her gently, almost like a father. He wants Ellie to stay with him: they're alike, he says — they both have a violent heart. Ellie grabs his hand and breaks his finger. And she finally tells him her name — so everyone will know it was Ellie who broke his finger. Meanwhile, Joel comes to. Barely able to stand, he sets off to find her. He catches two of David's men and makes them talk. He interrogates them so brutally that it's genuinely frightening. Once he gets his answer, he leaves neither of them alive. To get Ellie back, Joel will do anything. Meanwhile, Ellie is being dragged to the butcher's table. At the last moment, she screams that she's infected and shows them her bite. James hesitates for a split second — and that's all she needs. Ellie kills him and breaks free. David is right behind her. The chase ends in an old restaurant that goes up in flames. David calls to her softly, almost singing, and that makes it even more terrifying. Among the burning tables, David hunts her like prey. Ellie wounds him with her knife, but he's much stronger. David pins her down and starts to strangle her. Ellie's fingers find a machete. She strikes — again and again, even after David is dead. Someone's hands pull her away. Ellie fights back, not realizing who it is. It's Joel. He holds her close and whispers that everything is all right — calling her "baby girl." That's what he used to call Sarah. In that moment, he stops seeing Ellie as cargo once and for all. She has become his daughter.

Spring. Joel and Ellie reach Salt Lake City. Somewhere here, at St. Mary's Hospital, the Fireflies should be waiting for them. Ellie seems like a different person: she barely talks, hardly jokes, and stares off into space for long stretches. She has been through things no child should ever have to go through. Now it's Joel's turn to try to get her talking. The roles have reversed: now he's the one cracking jokes and telling stories, while she stays silent. And suddenly, Ellie spots something and rushes ahead. Giraffes. Ellie freezes, unable to believe her eyes. Real giraffes that escaped from a zoo long ago, wandering freely through the ruins. Ellie pets one of them and, for the first time in a long while, truly smiles. It's one of the brightest scenes in the game: in the middle of a dead world, life still goes on. They watch for a long time as the herd wanders off through the green city. Joel says they could turn around and go back to Tommy's. They don't have to see this through. But Ellie says that after everything they've been through, it can't all be for nothing. And so they keep going. Joel, it seems, was hoping for a different answer. The way to the hospital leads through a flooded tunnel. Ellie can't swim, so Joel pushes her ahead of him on a wooden pallet. The current drags Ellie under. Joel dives in and pulls her out, but she isn't breathing. He tries to bring her back — and at that moment, a Firefly patrol surrounds them. A rifle butt to the head — and darkness.

Joel wakes up in a hospital room. Marlene is standing beside him. Ellie is alive: she's here, in the Fireflies' hospital. But what Marlene says next is something Joel is not prepared for. The fungus inside Ellie has mutated, and that's exactly why it doesn't kill her. The Fireflies' scientists have been searching for someone like her for years. To make a vaccine, the doctors have to remove the fungus directly from her brain. Ellie won't survive the surgery. No one even asked her. Marlene is sure Ellie would choose this herself — after all, the survival of humanity is at stake. For the Fireflies, it's a sacrifice for everyone else on Earth. It's hard for Marlene too: long ago, she promised Ellie's mother she would look after her. But the decision has been made. Joel is escorted out under guard. He has no intention of leaving. Joel disarms the guard and starts fighting his way through the hospital. The game gives us no choice: we are Joel, and we have to walk this path with him. Floor after floor, he kills everyone who gets in his way. He bursts into the operating room. Ellie lies under anesthesia, unaware of anything. A surgeon stands in front of Ellie, holding up a scalpel. Joel kills him. He unhooks Ellie from the machines and carries her out in his arms. Behind him, an alarm is blaring. And for her, he was ready to burn the whole world down.

Ellie wakes up in the back seat of a car. She has no idea what happened. Joel tells her there turned out to be lots of others like her — dozens. That the vaccine didn't work, and the Fireflies gave up looking. That they're going home. It's all a lie. Joel is convinced it's better for her this way. Ellie says nothing and stares out the window for a long time. They walk the last few miles to Tommy's settlement on foot. The sun is shining, and ahead lies the valley where Tommy, Maria, and a peaceful life are waiting for them. Suddenly, Ellie stops. And she tells Joel about the night she was bitten, together with Riley. Riley died first. Then Tess. Then Sam. Everyone died, but she's still here — still waiting for her turn. Joel tells her none of it is her fault. He admits that for years he was just surviving — but she, no matter what, always found something to fight for. Ellie looks him in the eye and asks him to swear that everything he told her about the Fireflies is true. Joel doesn't look away. He could tell her the truth right now. "I swear." Ellie is silent for a long moment. And then she answers: "Okay." And with that one word, it all ends.

The game never tells us whether Joel was right. He lied to the person he loves more than anything in the world. He made the choice for Ellie — and maybe robbed humanity of a cure. Or maybe not: no one knows whether that vaccine would ever have worked. But remember how it all began: twenty years ago, his daughter died in his arms. And now fate has put him in front of the same choice again. This time, he chose his girl. Even if it meant lying to her. Remember the family on the roadside, on the first night of the outbreak? Joel has always chosen his own, even if it meant leaving the rest of the world behind. So who is he: a hero who saved a child, or a selfish man who took away the world's last hope? Everyone has to answer that question for themselves. And that's exactly why people love The Last of Us: it isn't a story about zombies. It's a story about love, about loss, and about how far a person will go for the ones they care about. And Ellie?
